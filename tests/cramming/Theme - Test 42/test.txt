What does the text most strongly suggest about how analyzing a painting with nonvisible light can inform a judgment about who that painting is attributed to?

Because they penetrate the visible surface of a painting, ultraviolet and other nonvisible spectra of light enable the detection of otherwise imperceptible features of the work. Beneath the surface of Michelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio’s *The Cardsharps* (ca. 1594), art historians found that Caravaggio had originally positioned the figure on the right side of the painting. Furthermore, nonvisible spectra can be used for chemical analysis to identify specific pigments and thus, in some cases, reveal forgeries: if, for example, titanium oxide—which wasn’t developed until the 20th century—is evident throughout the layers of a painting attributed to an 18th-century artist, that work can only be a forgery.

Question 18:
Which statement about the difrasismo in cuāuhtli in ocēlōtl is most strongly supported by the text?

Poetry in Classical Nahuatl, the language of the Aztec Empire, relies on difrasismo, or a parallel noun construction that conventionally operates as a single metaphor. For example, the common difrasismo in cuāuhtli in ocēlōtl (literally, "the eagle, the jaguar") signifies "warrior." The device's function is both formal—providing structure to lines of verse—and ritual: semantic relations among the two nouns and the concept they signify can be tenuous, as in the previous example, such that difrasismos are often only intelligible according to the conceptual associations observed in Aztec ceremonial culture.

D. Its metaphorical significance derives from the semantic equivalence of the two nouns constituting the difrasismo.
